Takeaways

What the data tells us

Denmark's food safety system — built around the public Smiley scheme and regular unannounced inspections — produces a broadly reassuring picture. Across 165,478 inspection records and 51,715 unique businesses, the vast majority pass cleanly. But looking below the surface reveals where the risk actually concentrates, what kind of businesses struggle most, and how effectively the system corrects problems when they arise.

The headline number is striking: 97.9% of businesses receive a Score 1 on their most recent inspection — no remarks, nothing to fix. That is a high baseline. It tells you that routine enforcement is working, and that most food businesses in Denmark take compliance seriously. The Smiley score displayed on every restaurant window and shop door creates real accountability: a visible, public signal that has shaped behaviour across the industry over the past decade.

But that average masks a lot of variation. The single strongest predictor of a bad inspection is not where a business is located — it is what kind of business it is. Retail shops and grocery stores are consistently among the highest-risk categories, while wholesale distributors and food contact material companies sit far below average. Within restaurants specifically, the type of cuisine served correlates with inspection outcomes too — though that analysis carries an important caveat, since cuisines are identified from business names rather than any official register.

The system broadly works. 97.9% of all active businesses pass their most recent inspection with no remarks. The enforcement infrastructure — inspections, public scores, re-inspections — is functioning at scale.
Business type is the dominant risk factor. Retail & Shops are 43% more likely than the average business to receive a serious outcome. Wholesale and food contact businesses are consistently the safest. Geography matters far less than what you sell.
Within restaurants, cuisine type adds nuance. Italian and Indian restaurants show risk levels above 3× the restaurant average. Kebab/Shawarma, Burger, Pizza, and Thai cluster around 1.7–1.8×. Sushi/Japanese is the only cuisine below the average at 0.80×. This should be read as exploratory — cuisines are identified by name keywords, not an official register.
Large operators tend to lead on compliance. Among the twelve operators with the most locations in Denmark, nine achieved a 100% clean record across every branch on their most recent inspection. Scale brings systems, training, and resources — and it shows up in the data.
Getting caught tends to lead to improvement. Of the 12,970 businesses that received a Score 2, 3, or 4 at one inspection, 88.8% had fully resolved the issue by their next visit. The enforcement cycle is not just punitive — it is corrective.
A note on what this data cannot tell us
The Smiley dataset stores only the four most recent inspections per business, which means long-running businesses have a shorter visible history than their actual record. Businesses that have closed are not always visible in the data. The cuisine classification is name-based and imprecise — a restaurant called "Indian Kitchen" is flagged as Indian; one called "Taj" is not. And relative risk figures compare groups to each other, but do not tell you anything about why certain types of businesses struggle more — that would require talking to inspectors, business owners, and regulators. The data shows the pattern. The explanation for it lies elsewhere.

What stands out most, stepping back from the individual findings, is how functional and transparent this system is. The Smiley scheme is public, searchable, and updated regularly. The data used here is openly available through Fødevarestyrelsen's API. That kind of institutional openness makes analyses like this possible — and makes it harder for problems to persist unnoticed. Whether you are a consumer choosing where to eat, a business owner benchmarking against competitors, or a regulator allocating inspection resources, the data is there to be used.

Data: Fødevarestyrelsen (Danish Veterinary and Food Administration) — Smiley inspection database, 2012–2026.
